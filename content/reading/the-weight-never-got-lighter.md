I have been thinking about something lately.

How much can a human being get used to?

Imagine you start lifting a 10 kg dumbbell every day. The first few days, it hurts. You feel every kilogram of it. Your hands hurt, your muscles hurt, and you probably complain about it.

Then you keep doing it.

A few weeks pass. Maybe a few months.

Eventually, you pick up that same dumbbell without thinking that much about it anymore.

But here is the thing.

The dumbbell is still 10 kg.

The weight did not decrease.

You just became better at carrying it.

And I think we do the exact same thing with the problems around us.

We get used to corruption. We get used to discrimination. We get used to bad systems. We get used to people in power not doing what they are supposed to do. We complain at first, then we find alternatives, then those alternatives become part of our lifestyle.

And maybe the scariest part is not that these things keep happening.

It is that we don't even find them strange anymore.

We are extremely good at coping

Look at what is happening with electricity.

The electricity goes regularly?

Okay. Buy a rechargeable fan.

Still a problem?

Buy rechargeable lights.

Have some more money?

Buy an IPS, inverter, generator, solar setup or whatever backup system you can afford.

Internet goes down with electricity? Keep mobile data. Charge the power bank. Download things beforehand. Arrange your work according to when electricity might be available.

Problem solved, right?

No.

You learned how to survive the problem. You did not solve it.

And there is a huge difference between those two things.

Someone who can afford an inverter may sit comfortably under a fan during load-shedding and slowly stop thinking about the fact that load-shedding is happening at all.

But they are paying for that comfort.

They paid for the inverter. They pay for the battery. Someone else pays for generator fuel. Businesses pay for backup power. A student without those resources pays with lost study hours. A small business might pay with lost production. Somewhere else, the consequences can be much more serious.

We are individually spending money and energy to compensate for something that functioning national infrastructure is supposed to provide reliably.

And because we have become so good at coping with it, we are creating a bubble around ourselves where everything somehow feels okay.

Electricity went out.

But my fan is running.

So, okay.

But why is that okay?

Why did we reach a point where the first thought is about buying another device instead of asking why we need that device in the first place?

And then we move on to the next trend

There is another part of this that bothers me.

Our attention changes unbelievably fast.

One serious issue is everywhere today. Tomorrow there is another controversy. Then another viral video. Then another trend.

Right now, for example, people are generating pictures of themselves imagining what they would look like in the 1980s. I have participated in trends too. There is nothing inherently wrong with having fun on the internet.

The problem begins when our attention span for national problems becomes shorter than our attention span for trends.

We see a serious article.

Scroll.

A report about infrastructure.

Scroll.

Someone talking about corruption or accountability.

Scroll.

Then something entertaining appears, and suddenly that gets our full attention.

The national problem did not disappear when we scrolled past it.

We just stopped looking at it.

And this is where I keep thinking about Mohamed Bouazizi and Abu Sayed.

Why does somebody have to die before we look?

Tunisia had problems before Mohamed Bouazizi.

Corruption did not suddenly appear on 17 December 2010. Economic frustration did not begin that morning. People did not discover humiliation, unemployment or abuse of authority because one street vendor protested.

People had been living with those things.

Then Bouazizi set himself on fire.

He survived initially but died from his injuries on 4 January 2011. His act became a catalyst for protests that brought down Tunisia's government and helped ignite what became known as the Arab Spring.

The problems were already there.

Something horrifying simply made those problems impossible to ignore.

And when I look at Bangladesh, I cannot avoid seeing a similar pattern—not an identical history, but a similar human reaction.

Before July 2024, Bangladesh had spent roughly fifteen and a half continuous years under Sheikh Hasina's Awami League government. Over those years, concerns about repression of political opposition, restrictions on expression and other abuses had been repeatedly documented.

But ordinary people also learned how to live inside that environment.

That is what humans do.

You know which things you probably should not say. You know which people have influence. You hear allegations of corruption and abuse and eventually another story does not shock you the way the first one did.

Something abnormal becomes familiar.

Then the quota protests began.

At first, they were protests over government-job quotas. Students protested. Violence followed. UN experts reported that people associated with the Bangladesh Chhatra League attacked protesters on 15 July, including students who were seeking treatment.

Again, another protest.

Another clash.

Another headline.

And then, on 16 July, Abu Sayed stood in front of police in Rangpur.

The video is difficult to reduce to politics because you can see a human being standing there.

The UN later described footage showing Abu Sayed standing still with his arms spread before police fired directly toward his chest and continued firing.

He died.

And suddenly something changed.

The quota issue did not remain only about quotas. The killing became one of the defining images of a much larger confrontation over state power and accountability. The protests eventually developed into a nationwide movement demanding Sheikh Hasina's removal, and her government fell on 5 August 2024.

Again, the problems did not magically appear when Abu Sayed died.

We finally looked at them differently.

And that is what scares me.

Because we have seen versions of this before.

We have been here before

Go back further.

1952. 

Bengalis were demanding recognition of Bangla as a state language of Pakistan. Students and others protested. On 21 February, students were killed in Dhaka during demonstrations—a sacrifice now commemorated globally through International Mother Language Day.

Again, people had grievances before somebody died.

Again, injustice existed before blood was spilled.

Again, death made it impossible to look away.

So I keep coming back to one question:

Why do we have to wait?

Why does somebody have to die before a problem becomes serious enough for everyone?

Why does there have to be a photograph?

Why does there have to be a video?

Why does something have to become so cruel, so visual and so undeniable that scrolling past it becomes difficult?

Why can we not recognize the 10 kg while we are still carrying it?

Maybe we don't need another one

Maybe we don't need another Abu Sayed.

Maybe we don't need another Mohamed Bouazizi.

Maybe we don't need another student dying in front of a camera, another national tragedy, another economic disaster or another horrifying event before we decide that something deserves our attention.

And no, the answer is not that everyone needs to stop buying rechargeable fans.

Buy the fan. You need to live your life.

Buy the light. Protect your work. Keep your power bank charged. Get an inverter if you need one.

But don't confuse coping with solving.

Use the fan, but still ask why the electricity keeps disappearing.

Use the inverter, but remember that you are paying additional money because the original system is failing you.

Use mobile data when the Wi-Fi stops, but still ask why reliable connectivity is unavailable.

Adapt when you have to.

Just don't normalize what should never have become normal.

And this applies to much more than electricity.

Question corruption before another scandal exposes it.

Question discrimination before someone has to become its symbol.

Question institutions before their failures become disasters.

Question people in power while the problem still looks small.

And question ourselves when we become too comfortable carrying something that should have been put down a long time ago.

People in positions of public power are not doing citizens a personal favor by providing functioning institutions and essential services.

That is the responsibility attached to the position.

Power should never make someone forget whom that power is supposed to serve.

And citizens should not let them forget either.

Maybe being conscious does not always mean standing in the middle of a road.

Sometimes it starts much earlier than that.

It starts when something happens for the hundredth time and, instead of saying—

“This is normal.”

—we finally ask:

“Why have I accepted this as normal?”

Because the dumbbell is still 10 kg.

We have just become frighteningly good at carrying it.
